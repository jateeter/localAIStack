# Recovery Protocols and Training Load Management

## Why recovery matters as much as training

Physiological adaptation — stronger muscles, higher VO2max, improved metabolic efficiency — happens during recovery, not during exercise. Training provides the stimulus; sleep and rest deliver the adaptation. Without adequate recovery time, training load accumulates as fatigue without the corresponding fitness gains, a state known as functional overreaching (short term) or non-functional overreaching (longer term).

## The three pillars of recovery

### 1. Sleep (primary driver)

Sleep is the most potent recovery intervention available. During slow-wave (deep) sleep, human growth hormone (HGH) peaks and drives muscle protein synthesis and tissue repair. During REM sleep, the nervous system consolidates motor patterns and regulates emotional responses to stress.

**Minimum for athletic recovery:** 8 hours.
**Optimal for heavy training blocks:** 9–10 hours.

A single night of 6 or fewer hours impairs reaction time, decision-making, and pain tolerance to a degree comparable to 24 hours without sleep.

### 2. Nutrition timing

- **Within 30 minutes of training:** 20–40 g protein + rapidly digested carbohydrate (3:1 carb:protein ratio for endurance athletes, 2:1 for strength athletes). This window maximises muscle protein synthesis and replenishes glycogen.
- **Evening meal:** lean protein, complex carbohydrates, colourful vegetables. Avoid heavy fat and alcohol within 3 hours of bed — both fragment sleep architecture.
- **Hydration:** aim for urine colour of pale yellow. Sweat losses of 1–2% body mass impair cognitive performance; losses > 2% impair physical performance.

### 3. Active recovery

Low-intensity movement (20–30 min walk, gentle cycling, yoga, swimming) on rest days promotes blood flow to muscle tissue, enhances metabolite clearance, and maintains parasympathetic nervous system tone without adding training stress.

## Heart rate variability (HRV) as a recovery readiness marker

HRV is the most sensitive real-time marker of autonomic recovery status available to consumer wearables. In practice:

| HRV relative to 7-day baseline | Interpretation | Recommended action |
|---|---|---|
| > 10% above baseline | High readiness — nervous system is well regulated | Full training intensity is safe |
| Within ±10% of baseline | Moderate readiness — normal daily variation | Proceed with planned training |
| 10–20% below baseline | Moderate fatigue | Reduce intensity by 20%; no new PRs |
| > 20% below baseline | Significant fatigue | Active recovery or full rest day |

## Periodisation and recovery weeks

For any structured training program lasting more than 4–6 weeks, scheduled recovery weeks are essential. A common model:

- **3:1 block**: 3 weeks progressive loading, 1 week reduced volume (50–60% of peak load). HRV typically recovers to or above baseline during the easy week.
- **2:1 block**: used during high-volume training camps or heavy competition schedules.
- **Deload cues**: if HRV is trending downward for 5+ consecutive days despite normal sleep, insert an unscheduled recovery week regardless of the plan.

## Recovery interventions with strong evidence

| Intervention | Mechanism | Evidence quality |
|---|---|---|
| Cold water immersion (10–15°C, 10–15 min) | Reduces inflammatory markers, decreases perceived soreness | Strong (particularly for repeat-bout endurance/team sports) |
| Contrast therapy (hot/cold alternation) | Vasodilation/vasoconstriction cycle clears metabolites | Moderate |
| Compression garments | Reduces oedema, enhances venous return | Moderate |
| Massage / foam rolling | Reduces DOMS, improves ROM | Moderate |
| Napping (20–30 min) | Restores cognitive performance, reduces cortisol | Strong |
| Meditation / breathing practice | Shifts autonomic balance toward parasympathetic | Moderate |

Interventions with weaker or mixed evidence: cryotherapy chambers, infrared saunas (some positive signals for HRV but limited RCTs), vibration therapy.

## Red flags that require medical evaluation

- Persistent HRV suppression (> 2 weeks) despite adequate rest and nutrition
- Resting HR consistently 10+ bpm above normal
- Unexplained performance decline lasting more than 2 weeks
- Sleep disturbance not attributable to environment or schedule changes
- Mood disturbances (irritability, depression) concurrent with training overload

These may indicate overtraining syndrome, thyroid dysfunction, iron deficiency, or infection — all of which require clinical assessment rather than a rest week alone.
