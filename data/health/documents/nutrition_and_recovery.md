# Nutrition for Recovery and Cognitive Performance

## The connection between nutrition, HRV, and health state

Nutritional status influences all three localAIStack health metrics:

- **Heart rate**: Dehydration elevates resting HR. Caffeine raises HR for 4–6 hours. Low iron causes compensatory tachycardia.
- **HRV**: Alcohol suppresses HRV for 24–48 hours even at moderate doses. Caloric restriction (>20% below maintenance) depresses HRV within days. High glycaemic index meals produce sympathetic activation and modest acute HRV reduction.
- **Sleep duration and quality**: Alcohol fragments sleep architecture (reduces deep NREM). Large pre-bed meals activate digestion and fragment sleep. Magnesium and tryptophan support melatonin synthesis and sleep onset.

## Protein and muscle recovery

**Recommended daily intake (active adults):**
- Resistance training: 1.6–2.2 g protein per kg body weight
- Endurance training: 1.2–1.8 g protein per kg body weight
- During weight loss or injury: upper end of ranges (2.0–2.5 g/kg to preserve lean mass)

**Quality sources:**
- Animal: eggs (complete amino acid profile), chicken/turkey breast, salmon (adds omega-3), Greek yoghurt
- Plant: soy (complete), lentils + rice combination (complementary amino acids), pea protein isolate, hemp seeds

**Timing**: distribute intake across 4–5 meals (20–40 g per meal) rather than concentrating protein in one meal. Muscle protein synthesis is maximised with leucine-rich meals every 3–4 hours during waking hours.

## Carbohydrates: fuel for brain and muscle

Carbohydrates are the primary fuel for the brain and high-intensity exercise. Chronic low carbohydrate intake impairs:
- Working memory and executive function
- Training quality in glycolytic exercise (intervals, team sports, lifting)
- Mood regulation and stress tolerance

Carbohydrate requirements are highly variable by activity level. Sedentary adults may thrive on 3–5 g/kg/day; elite endurance athletes may require 8–12 g/kg/day on training days.

**Pre-workout (1–3 hours before)**: moderate carbohydrate + moderate protein. Avoid high fat or high fibre in this window (both delay gastric emptying).

**Post-workout (within 30–45 min)**: high carbohydrate + protein. A 3:1 carb:protein ratio optimises glycogen resynthesis for next-day training readiness.

## Fats: essential for hormonal health and inflammation

Omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA from fatty fish, or ALA from flaxseed/chia converted less efficiently):
- Anti-inflammatory; reduce delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS)
- Support cell membrane integrity and receptor sensitivity
- Improve vagal tone and HRV when supplemented at 2–4 g EPA+DHA/day in deficient individuals

Saturated fat intake above 15% of total calories is associated with reduced insulin sensitivity and increased inflammatory markers — both HRV suppressors over time.

## Micronutrients that affect HRV and heart rate

| Nutrient | Role | Deficiency effects |
|---|---|---|
| Magnesium | Cofactor for 300+ enzymes; key for cardiac conduction | Elevated resting HR, muscle cramps, poor sleep, low HRV |
| Iron | Oxygen transport (haemoglobin) | Compensatory tachycardia, fatigue, reduced aerobic capacity |
| Vitamin D | Immune regulation, cardiometabolic health | Linked to lower HRV in large observational studies |
| B vitamins (B12, folate) | Homocysteine regulation, nerve function | B12 deficiency causes elevated HR, palpitations (rare but significant) |
| Potassium | Cardiac action potential | Low potassium → arrhythmia risk, elevated HR |
| Sodium (electrolytes) | Fluid balance, blood pressure regulation | Low sodium → lightheadedness, elevated HR on standing |

## Hydration

**Resting requirement**: 2–3 L/day for most adults (varies with body size, climate, activity).

**Sweat losses**: an active person in a warm environment can lose 1–2 L/hour. Thirst is a lagging indicator — by the time you're thirsty, you may already have lost 1–1.5% body mass as fluid.

**HR effects of dehydration**:
- 1–2% body mass loss: HR +3–5 bpm at rest
- 3–5% body mass loss: HR +8–15 bpm; significant performance impairment
- >5%: severe impairment; heat illness risk

**Electrolyte replacement**: for exercise > 90 min or heavy sweating, plain water is insufficient. Include sodium (500–1000 mg/L), potassium (150–200 mg/L), and magnesium (60–100 mg/L) in recovery hydration.

## Alcohol and its effects on health metrics

Alcohol is one of the most potent acute HRV suppressors. Even 1–2 drinks:
- Reduces overnight HRV by 10–20% vs. non-drinking baseline
- Increases resting HR by 3–8 bpm the following morning
- Fragments sleep: more awakenings, less deep NREM and REM
- Effects peak 6–8 hours after consumption (next-morning HRV reflects previous night's consumption)

For athletes tracking recovery: a single evening of moderate drinking (2–3 standard drinks) commonly shifts the health state from "thriving" to "watch" the following day.

## Caffeine

Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors, producing alertness and acutely elevating heart rate by 3–8 bpm. The half-life is 5–6 hours in most adults, meaning caffeine consumed at 2 pm still reduces sleep quality at midnight.

**Evidence-based guidance**:
- Stop caffeine by noon (or 6 hours before bed) to minimise sleep disruption
- Recommended daily upper limit: 400 mg (approx. 4 standard espresso shots)
- Avoid caffeine in the 2 hours after waking — adenosine pressure during this period supports sleep debt recovery. Delay first coffee by 60–90 minutes for better afternoon energy.

## The "attention" state and nutrition

When localAIStack reports an "attention" health state (HR outside nominal band), common nutritional contributors include:
- Dehydration (most common reversible cause of elevated HR)
- Caffeine or stimulant excess
- Inadequate caloric intake (chronic mild restriction elevates HR long term)
- Iron deficiency anaemia (gradual onset, compensatory tachycardia)
- Electrolyte imbalance (especially potassium, sodium)

The localAI assistant in attention state will gently suggest hydration and inquire whether unusual dietary patterns might be contributing before suggesting medical evaluation.
